## THEORY
heading: 2.0  Opening the hood a little further
body:
In Chapter 1 you learned that an LLM is a next-chunk-of-text predictor. This chapter opens the hood a little further — not to turn you into a machine-learning researcher, but to explain the three practical ideas that account for most of an LLM's real-world behavior: TOKENS (how it reads and writes), the CONTEXT WINDOW (how much it can hold in mind at once), and TEMPERATURE (how predictable or creative it is).

Understanding these three explains a surprising amount: why LLMs cost what they cost, why they sometimes forget, why they cut off mid-answer, and why they are sometimes wildly inconsistent.
callout: Three ideas — tokens, context window, temperature — explain most of what an LLM does in the real world.

## THEORY
heading: 2.1  Tokens — the LLM does not see words
body:
It is natural to assume an LLM reads text the way you do — as words and letters. It does not. Internally, text is broken into TOKENS: small chunks that are often whole words, but sometimes pieces of words, punctuation, or spaces. This chopping step is called "tokenization."

A rough feel for the sizes (for English): 1 token is roughly 4 characters, or about three-quarters of a word. 100 tokens is roughly 75 words — a short paragraph.

A single common word like "cat" is usually one token. A longer or rarer word like "unbelievable" might be split into a few tokens, such as "un", "believ", and "able." Numbers, code, and non-English text get chopped differently, often into more tokens.

Why should you care about tokens at all? Because almost every practical property of working with LLMs is measured in tokens. COST: providers charge per token — both the tokens you send IN and the tokens the model produces OUT. Free tiers (like the ones you would use to learn) limit you by tokens as well. LIMITS: the model can only handle so many tokens at once (see 2.2). SPEED: producing more tokens takes more time, because the model generates them one at a time.

So "tokens" is the unit of currency, the unit of capacity, and the unit of time all at once. Whenever later chapters talk about keeping prompts short or trimming history, the deep reason is always the same: tokens are limited and costly.
callout: Tokens are the unit of currency, of capacity, and of time — all at once.

## THEORY
heading: 2.2  The context window — the LLM's desk, not its filing cabinet
body:
The CONTEXT WINDOW is the maximum amount of text — measured in tokens — that the model can consider at one time. It includes BOTH what you send in AND what the model writes back. If a conversation grows longer than the window, something has to give (older material must be dropped or summarized — Chapter 9).

In 2026, context windows vary widely. Older or smaller models might hold only a few thousand tokens; larger, modern ones hold hundreds of thousands, and the biggest can hold a million or more. Bigger windows let the model consider more at once (a whole document, a long conversation), but they also cost more and can be slower.

Now the consequence that shapes half of agent engineering. Read it carefully: the model has NO memory between requests. Each request is completely independent — like a function that keeps nothing after it returns.

If you want the model to "remember" what was said a moment ago, YOU must include that earlier text again in the next request. The sense that you are having a continuous conversation is an ILLUSION that the surrounding software creates by re-sending the history every time.

A useful comparison for you: an LLM request is like calling a stateless REST endpoint. The server holds no session. If it needs to know what came before, the client must send that context along with every call. Conversation "memory" works exactly the same way — the history rides along on each request.

A good mental model: treat the context window as the model's DESK, not its filing cabinet. Whatever you want it to consider must be laid out on the desk right now. Anything not on the desk simply does not exist for this request. This single fact is why memory systems (Chapter 9) and retrieval (Chapters 10-11) exist at all.
callout: The context window is the model's DESK, not its filing cabinet — and it is wiped clean the instant each request finishes.

## THEORY
heading: 2.3  How generation actually feels — one token at a time
body:
Recall from Chapter 1 that the model builds output by predicting one small piece at a time. Concretely: the model looks at all the tokens so far, produces a ranked set of possibilities for the very next token, one is chosen, it is added to the text, and the whole thing repeats. Hundreds of these tiny steps produce a paragraph.

Two practical effects fall out of this. "Streaming": because tokens are produced one by one, software can show them to you as they arrive — that live "typing" effect in chat apps. It is not a gimmick; it reflects how generation genuinely works.

Getting cut off: the model does not know in advance how long its answer will be. If a limit on output length is reached before it is finished, the answer simply stops mid-thought. When later chapters mention a "maximum output length," this is why it matters — set it too low and answers get truncated.
callout: Streaming and mid-sentence cut-offs both follow directly from one fact: the model writes one token at a time.

## THEORY
heading: 2.4  Temperature — the predictable-to-creative dial
body:
When the model considers the next token, it does not have just one candidate — it has many, each with a probability. TEMPERATURE is the setting that decides how adventurously it picks among them.

Temperature near 0.0: the model almost always picks the single most likely next token. Output becomes highly consistent and predictable — ask the same thing twice and you'll get nearly identical answers. It can feel a little flat, but it is RELIABLE.

Temperature around 0.7: a balanced middle. The model sometimes chooses a less-likely token, producing more varied, natural, "creative" text. A common default for casual chat.

Temperature around 1.0 and above: the model takes more chances. Output becomes more surprising and diverse — and more error-prone and off-topic.

There is a related dial you may hear about, "top-p" (nucleus sampling), which limits the pool of candidates to the most probable ones. For understanding purposes, just know it is another knob affecting randomness; temperature is the one you will hear about most.

THE KEY LESSON FOR AGENTS: for an agent's DECISION-MAKING, you almost always want LOW temperature (near 0). You want the agent to choose actions consistently and predictably, not creatively. An agent deciding "which tool should I use?" should behave more like a careful clerk than an improvising artist.
callout: For agent decisions, keep temperature LOW. Save creativity for genuinely creative tasks.

## THEORY
heading: 2.5  Why LLMs hallucinate (and why it's not a bug you can fix)
body:
"Hallucination" is when a model produces something fluent, confident, and false. Given everything above, you can now see WHY it happens: the model's only job is to produce plausible-sounding text. "Plausible" and "true" usually overlap — but not always. When the model does not actually know something, it does not fall silent; it generates the most likely-looking continuation, which may be a convincing fabrication.

This is not a defect that a future patch will remove. It is inherent to what a text-prediction machine is. That is precisely why the field leans so heavily on: TOOLS (Chapter 6) to fetch real facts instead of guessing them; RETRIEVAL / RAG (Chapter 11) to ground answers in real documents; EVALUATION (Chapter 15) to measure how often the model is wrong; and GUARDRAILS (Chapter 16) to catch bad output before it reaches a user.

The takeaway: never assume an LLM's factual claim is correct simply because it sounds authoritative. Design so that anything that MUST be right is checked, retrieved, or computed — not merely generated.
callout: Hallucination is inherent, not a bug. Anything that MUST be right should be checked, retrieved, or computed — never merely generated.

## THEORY
heading: 2.6  Model size and choice (a first look)
body:
Models come in different sizes, usually described by their number of internal parameters (the tunable numbers inside the neural network). Larger models tend to be more capable at hard reasoning but are slower and more expensive. Smaller models are faster and cheaper and are often perfectly good — sometimes better — for the many small, quick decisions an agent makes.

You do not need to memorize model names; they change constantly, and by the time you read this some will be gone and others born. What endures is the JUDGMENT: match the model to the job. Use a small, fast model for routine decisions inside a loop; reserve a large model for the genuinely difficult step. For now, simply retire the instinct that "biggest is best" — in agent work, it usually is not.
callout: Match the model to the job. "Biggest is best" is the wrong instinct for agents.

## THEORY
heading: 2.7  Putting the three ideas together
body:
These three concepts interlock. TOKENS are the unit of everything — cost, capacity, and time. The CONTEXT WINDOW is how many tokens fit on the desk at once, and it resets to empty every request (no built-in memory). TEMPERATURE controls how predictably the model turns its input tokens into output tokens.

Now several everyday observations make sense at once. A long conversation gets more expensive with each turn because you resend a growing history (more input tokens every time). An answer stops abruptly because it hit an output-length limit (ran out of allowed tokens). Two identical questions give different answers because the temperature was not near zero. And a confident wrong answer appears because the model optimizes for plausible, not true. None of this is mysterious once you hold these three ideas in mind.
callout: Tokens, context, temperature — hold these three in mind and the LLM's odd behaviors stop being mysterious.
